While I was building a [visualisation of the threat scenarios](/threat-scenarios/) in Finland's National Risk Assessment 2026, I started to wonder how the other Nordic and Baltic countries describe their national risks. A quick search showed that all eight – Finland, Sweden, Norway, Denmark, Iceland, Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania – have published a list of the risks they assess. So I put them side by side: 254 risks, grouped into 39 shared themes. The result is the comparison [Nordic and Baltic risk assessments side by side](/risk-assessments/).

This post is about what the comparison shows, and about one thing it nearly hid.

## Publishing is a choice

The EU Civil Protection Mechanism asks the participating states to assess their disaster risks and to share a summary of the assessment with the European Commission every three years. It does not require them to publish anything. Still, every one of the eight has made its risk list public, from Denmark's 16 event types to Latvia's catalogue of 58 risks.

## A lot is shared

Six themes appear in all eight assessments: terrorist attacks, pandemics, maritime accidents and oil spills, wildfires, nuclear or radiological accidents, and power outages. Fifteen themes appear in at least six of them. That is hardly surprising, as the countries share a climate, a sea and, to a large extent, a threat environment.

## Seven themes belong to one country

Seven themes appear as a risk of their own in one assessment only:

- **Finland:** the availability of skilled labour in a crisis, and a disruption of public finances
- **Sweden:** a mass-casualty event abroad that affects people with ties to Sweden
- **Latvia:** social polarisation
- **Iceland:** environmental pollution, and a disruption of geothermal district heating
- **Lithuania:** damage to cultural heritage – in practice a single site, Gediminas Hill in Vilnius, where rain and thaw have caused landslides on the slopes since 2016

## What an empty cell does not tell

When I shared the first version, two comments asked exactly the right questions. Why would polarisation be a risk only in Latvia, when Estonia has a Russian-speaking population of a similar size? And why would Finland, of all countries, be the only one worried about the availability of skilled labour?

The answer was hiding in the cells that looked empty. In the comparison, an empty cell meant that the assessment had no separate risk for the theme. It did not mean that the assessment ignored the phenomenon. So I went back to the texts. For each of the seven one-country themes, I searched the other seven assessments for passages that treat the phenomenon as part of another risk, as a vulnerability or as a contributing factor. Of the 49 country–theme pairs, 32 had such a passage. Each of them is now marked in the comparison with a link to the page, and the supporting quotes have been checked against the source texts.

Polarisation is the clearest example. Only Latvia lists it as a risk, but six of the other seven assessments discuss it – most of them as a method or an aim of a hostile actor rather than as a societal risk in itself.

{% include ih-matrix.html number=2 figure=page.figures.polarisation %}

Staff shortages follow the same pattern. Finland is the only country with a risk of its own, but Sweden names staff shortage as one of four vulnerabilities that cut across many risks, Norway and Denmark discuss absences from work during a pandemic, Latvia treats the shortage of health-care staff as an amplifier, and Lithuania expects health-care institutions to lack staff after a nuclear accident.

The other one-country themes are not that unique either: pollution is mentioned in all seven other assessments, heating supply in six and cultural heritage in five. A disruption of public finances is the exception. Apart from Finland, only Iceland's assessment from 2011, written three years after the country's banking collapse, names a disruption of the state treasury's liquidity. And Denmark explicitly leaves out events that are unlikely in or near Denmark, even if they could affect Danes and Danish interests abroad.

## The difference is often in the framing

So the more interesting difference is not always what the countries see, but how they frame it. Whether polarisation is written down as a risk of its own, as a consequence of influence operations or as a method of a hostile actor shapes who is expected to own it, how it is followed and what is done about it. In my humble opinion, that is a question worth asking of any national risk picture, Finland's included.

## Caveats

The assessments are not like for like. Iceland's is from 2011, Norway's is a set of scenario analyses from 2019 onwards, and the purposes, methods and scales differ. The comparison shows what each country has written into its public assessment, not its actual level of risk or preparedness. Mapping the risks to themes is my own interpretation, the summaries were drafted with the help of AI and checked against the source pages, and the mentions were searched for the seven one-country themes only. The differences by country and the method are described on a [page of their own](/risk-assessments/method.html).

If you know one of these assessments well, please tell me what I got wrong. Corrections are listed on the same [method page](/risk-assessments/method.html#corrections).
