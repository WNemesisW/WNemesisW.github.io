iMethod and caveats
  • Shodan counts are totals of internet-visible services in Finland and the comparison countries. The queries were calibrated with Shodan's facets; queries whose hits could not be confirmed as the right product (FortiGate, MOVEit, Carel, Danfoss AK-SM and Kaseya VSA) were left out. Each query is linked to the part of the chain where the service is typically used; the same service may also be used elsewhere. Europe (the red figure) is a single query for 50 countries: the EU, the EEA, Switzerland, the UK, the Western Balkans, Ukraine, Moldova and the microstates and self-governing territories; Russia, Belarus, Turkey and the Caucasus are not included. Product queries were also calibrated against the European hits; the European SAP figure is not shown, because only a small share of its hits could be confirmed as SAP. No individual addresses, organisations or vulnerabilities are published.
  • The cases are publicly documented. Steps are numbered in the order the attack progressed, as far as the source describes it; a dashed line marks a step that is uncertain or only partly happened. Perpetrators are not shown, because the view is meant for targeting your own measures. Counts describe these cases only, not likelihood.

iMethod and caveats · Observations from the cases
  • The map is a simplified model of a typical power chain: generation, grid connection, main grid, substation and customers, plus the networks used to control the chain. The components drawn are those that one of the cases hit or passed through. The names are generic and do not depict any company's systems.
  • The cases are publicly documented cyberattacks that reached an electricity company, one of its facilities or a service provider in the chain. Each one's course is described in a publication by an authority, a CERT or the company itself. The list is not exhaustive.
  • The steps are numbered in the order the attack progressed, as far as the source describes it. A dashed line marks a step that is uncertain or happened at only some of the targets. Perpetrators are not shown, because the view is meant for targeting your own measures.
  • The counts describe these five cases only, not how common or likely attacks are.
  • Caveats: The Enercon figures are the company's statements as reported in the news. The metering service case is ongoing: the details come from a network company's notice, and the ransomware group's claims are not repeated. The remote disconnect requirement for new meters is from Finnish Energy's metering guidelines.
  • In four cases the attacker got in through a device or remote connection exposed to the internet: the company's perimeter, the satellite operator or the firewall at the grid connection. The route in the fifth case has not been disclosed.
  • The control room was affected in four: the attacker reached it, or its link to the field was cut.
  • In two, the attack hit a service provider rather than the electricity company: the satellite operator and the metering service supplier.
  • Power was cut only once, when the attacker got from the control room all the way to the substation.

iWhat the incidents show

Incidents in which a single supplier led to many organisations being hit. Red dot: impact on the sector confirmed; pale: minor or from news sources only.

  • The widest impact came through trusted update and remote management channels: NotPetya, SolarWinds, Kaseya, 3CX and CrowdStrike.
  • Healthcare most often failed through a non-clinical supplier: the laboratory, payments or a data centre.
  • Food retail stopped through the till supplier, not through production.
  • Supply chain incidents affecting power have been losses of monitoring, not blackouts.
  • No supply chain incident spreading across sectors was found for water. The gap may be real or a lack of reporting.
