Typical challenges in remote access to substations and generation plants

For power company management: where remote access challenges typically lie and why they arise.

The yellow line is the flow of electricity from the generation plant (solar power as the example) through the substation and the distribution grid to customers. Above it are the company's own functions and, above them on a level of their own, the external parties that need remote access to the substation or the plant. A party's area, lines and connection device share one colour. The external parties come over the internet, each to its own connection device, and the control room has its own telecontrol link to the connection devices of the substation and the plant. Each party's colour runs from its own connection device to the devices it needs. The threat actor at the top can attack any party. The numbered marks show where the four challenges described below sit.

NIS2 and the Finnish Cybersecurity Act

  • Management approves the cybersecurity risk-management model, oversees its implementation and is accountable for it (Cybersecurity Act section 10, NIS2 Art. 20).
  • An early notification of a significant incident must be made within 24 hours of detecting it (section 11, NIS2 Art. 23). Without an overall view, the incident is not detected in time.
  • In the electricity sector the supervisory authority is the Energy Authority.
  1. 1

    Many parties, many doors of their own

    Many parties besides the company itself need access to the substation and the plant, and each brings its own connection device: a firewall, a VPN endpoint, a router or a cloud link. The company owns the site but does not control its entry points.

    • Supplier: The automation vendor updates and troubleshoots the substation automation remotely, because often only its own staff may install updates.NCSC-FI: updates only by the vendor's staff; travel is expensive and slow
    • Substation: The automation vendor's firewall at the substation. The company cannot see its settings or know whether it is patched.Denmark 2023: some companies assumed the vendor handled updates, others skipped them because the vendor charged for installing them
    • Company IT: The company's own IT cannot see these devices. In Denmark some companies did not know the device existed: a supplier had installed a firewall with the cameras without telling them.
    • Manufacturer: The manufacturer can change settings and update software from the cloud. A targeted compromise of just 3 GW could significantly affect Europe's power grid, and over a dozen manufacturers control significantly more.SolarPower Europe and DNV 2025
    • Generation plant: The link runs from the manufacturer's cloud to the inverter. Researchers found 46 new vulnerabilities in three manufacturers' systems, some of which would have allowed taking over inverters through the cloud.Forescout 2025; the manufacturers fixed the findings

    Why this happens: Each delivery project brings its own connection and contract. Connections accumulate over the years and are never brought together in one place. The device is part of the supplier's service, and the contract does not say who patches it or who can see it.

    NIS2: supply chain security and asset management (Cybersecurity Act s. 9 items 4 and 5; NIS2 Art. 21(2)(d) and (i)).

  2. 2

    Every door leads everywhere

    The network is not divided within the layer, so every device in the connection layer has network-level access to every device in the layer below: relays, control, cameras and inverters. One mistake in one connection is enough for the whole site.

    • Substation: Control, protection relays, the operator panel and cameras sit in the same network. In Poland the devices were destroyed one by one in address order.Dragos: poor IT/OT segmentation in 81% of assessments
    • Substation: At the Polish facilities the network was divided into segments, but the attacker gained admin rights on the firewall and through them reached every segment.

    Why this happens: The substation was built to work, not to withstand an intruder. Dividing the network adds design and maintenance work and is often not required in procurement.

    NIS2: security of network and information systems and their maintenance (s. 9 items 2 and 3; NIS2 Art. 21(2)(a) and (e)).

  3. 3

    One key, many locks

    The same accounts at several facilities and no second factor: one leaked password opens many sites at once.

    • O&M: Reusing the same accounts and passwords across several facilities is common in the industry.CERT Polska 2026
    • Generation plant: The VPN accepted just a username and password. In Poland this was the case at every facility that was attacked.Dragos: 73% of all its incident response cases involved compromised VPN or jump host credentials
    • Substation: The devices still had the manufacturer's default accounts, which were used to destroy them.

    Why this happens: The same small team services dozens of sites. Shared accounts are quick, and older devices often lack a second factor.

    NIS2: access control and authentication, multi-factor authentication (s. 9 item 7; NIS2 Art. 21(2)(i) and (j)).

  4. 4

    No one sees the whole

    The control room sees the electricity and the suppliers see their own connections, but few can see in one place who has been on site and what was done. An anomaly is often noticed only when something stops working. Routers and monitoring devices are installed once and forgotten. They are often directly on the internet and unpatched.

    • Company IT: The company has no logs of the suppliers' connections. Only 46% of Dragos's assessments found adequate OT network monitoring.
    • Substation: The logs sit on the suppliers' devices. In Poland the attacker reset the devices to factory settings, and complete logs could not be recovered from any of them.CERT Polska 2026
    • Generation plant: Researchers found 86,000 industrial and critical infrastructure mobile routers visible on the internet, and fewer than 10% were confirmed to be patched.Forescout 2023
    • Generation plant: In Japan about 800 solar plant monitoring devices were taken over in 2024 through a known, already fixed vulnerability and used for online banking fraud. The plants' output was not disrupted.pv magazine 2024
    • Control room: The control room sees switching states and measurements, but not connections. Of Dragos's incident cases, 30% began with unexplained operational issues.Dragos 2026

    Why this happens: The device runs for years without attention and has no named owner. Updating it would need a site visit or downtime, so it is postponed. Monitoring was built for operating the grid. Security monitoring is split between suppliers, and no one brings it together.

    NIS2: incident detection and handling, and notification within 24 hours (s. 9 item 9, s. 11; NIS2 Art. 21(2)(b), Art. 23).
